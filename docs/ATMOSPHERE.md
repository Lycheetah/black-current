# Making Black Current feel like a place

Design proposals for Mac / Lycheetah's game. This is an ideas menu, not a list of
finished features or a commitment to build every item. Some ideas extend prototype
systems that already exist. Final art, lore, ship names and taste belong to Mac.

The strongest direction is **a small, strange salvage adventure with heavy arcade
combat**: a harbour you recognise, an ocean that changes as you travel, and a ship that
looks increasingly like the choices you made.

Atmosphere needs rhythm. Leave a safe place, notice something unusual, approach it,
understand just enough to act, survive a short burst of danger, then return with a story.
Quiet time needs meaningful navigation and discovery; it should not become waiting.

Keep the readable sector grid. Use seeds for encounters, routes through local debris,
salvage arrangements and signals inside those sectors. A fixed chart helps the player
form memories while the contents can surprise them. A larger procedural universe is not
necessary to make the current world feel alive.

## 1–10: Light, distance and the feeling of the ocean

1. **A light field with a purpose.** Give hazards, salvage and safe structures distinct
   light signatures. The player should learn what a light means before reading a label.

2. **A distant Black Sun.** Let its apparent position and colour change slowly between
   depths. Keep it a navigation and mood reference, never a screen-obscuring effect.

3. **Three layers of parallax.** Far haze, distant silhouettes and nearby drift establish
   distance with a few cached layers rather than expensive full-screen filters.

4. **Currents you can see.** Flecks and thin ribbons show local flow direction. A current
   can carry salvage or help a damaged ship limp home.

5. **A restrained palette per region.** Choose one ambient colour, one material colour
   and one danger accent. Save strong saturation for things the player must notice.

6. **Navigation lights on structures.** Rotating beacons and paired docking lights make
   stations readable as physical places, even when their labels are hidden.

7. **Depth through occlusion.** Pass behind large broken structures and under sparse
   hanging cables, with a clear outline when the ship would otherwise disappear.

8. **Movement wakes.** Give living hulls, industrial engines and unstable cores different
   trails. Restrict their length and density so the battlefield stays readable.

9. **Distant events.** A far-off flare, silent explosion or moving shadow suggests other
   things are happening beyond the immediate fight. Occasionally it becomes reachable.

10. **Optional visual intensity.** Separate particles, glow, distortion and shake controls.
    The atmosphere should survive at the lowest effect setting.

## 11–20: Sound that tells you where you are

11. **A harbour sound bed.** Warm machinery, restrained radio chatter and a soft recurring
    musical phrase establish safety. The same phrase can welcome a battered ship home.

12. **An engine with a personality.** Small hulls chatter; large assemblies rumble and
    resonate. Change the sound when a thruster is damaged or disconnected.

13. **A sound for each depth.** Give each region a different tonal centre and texture.
    The transition should be audible without needing more instruments or louder music.

14. **Sonar as an answer.** A ping gets different returns from metal, living mass and
    unusual signals. Build curiosity from the reply instead of relying on visual fog.

15. **Spatial distress calls.** The signal grows clearer as the player approaches its
    source. Reward listening with a useful bearing, not a mandatory audio puzzle.

16. **Heavy weapons with preparation.** A capacitor whine or breech mechanism precedes
    a capital shot. The sound gives the player time to react and makes the gun feel huge.

17. **Readable incoming fire.** Different dangerous projectile families have short,
    recognisable sounds. Avoid an indistinguishable wall of repeated weapon noise.

18. **Salvage with material.** Brass clinks, membrane pops and core resonance distinguish
    pickups. Let the sound reveal a valuable component before its inventory text appears.

19. **After-battle release.** Reduce the combat layer gradually, leaving engine and debris
    sounds. Make the moment of noticing that you survived feel distinct.

20. **Deliberate silence.** Occasionally remove a familiar ambient layer near something
    strange. Keep subtitles and visual equivalents for any information the player needs.

## 21–30: Places worth stopping at

21. **A recognisable home dock.** Build a small navigable harbour around the Shipyard,
    with a few fixed landmarks and a clear departure route.

22. **Modular stations.** Reuse a kit of docks, tanks, cranes, radiators and antennae.
    Different arrangements can make several places without commissioning whole stations.

23. **Wreck interiors with routes.** A dead capital contains gaps, hazards and salvage
    pockets. Small ships take tight routes; large ships can break a path through.

24. **A living reef.** Membrane growths close around debris and react to nearby movement.
    Breaking them may reveal salvage or attract organisms that were feeding there.

25. **An abandoned maintenance site.** Half-working repair machinery, a service light and
    one useful component tell a story without a page of exposition.

26. **A quiet trading stop.** A few immediate barter choices offer a reason to dock.
    Keep transactions fast and equipment-based rather than creating market paperwork.

27. **A war cathedral made from hulls.** Extend the Grave Choir with a few large,
    recognisable structural pieces. Let formation, sound and repetition carry the mood.

28. **A moving point of interest.** A slow salvage barge or migratory organism crosses
    the sector. Meeting it changes the route through an otherwise familiar place.

29. **Local landmarks on the chart.** Discoveries become recognisable symbols with a
    player note. The map gradually becomes a record of places the player knows.

30. **Visible repair and recovery.** Cleared or helped locations change a little when
    revisited: a light returns, a dock reopens, a wreck becomes a working outpost.

## 31–40: Your ship as a creature and a workshop

31. **A gentle first fitting.** Start with a useful hull and teach one meaningful choice.
    Let the player move a gun, see its arc and launch without reading a manual.

32. **Small assembly animations.** A clamp closes, a membrane stretches or a part settles
    into its socket. Keep the animation short and allow immediate editing.

33. **Visible reactor routing.** Brief pulses show which systems receive power when the
    player changes doctrine. Do not cover the hull with permanent diagnostic spaghetti.

34. **A live firing-range preview.** Test traverse, recoil and power draw from the editor.
    Use the actual weapon logic so the preview teaches what will happen in flight.

35. **Group fitting operations.** Select a wing or battery, mirror it, move it or replace
    its weapons as one action. Make large ship editing practical without changing the format.

36. **Damage with character.** Lost guns leave mounts; weakened membranes change shape.
    A damaged ship should remain recognisable as the same player-authored thing.

37. **Wear that stays optional.** Scorching, repair seams and mismatched replacement
    plates give a ship history. Offer a clean refit for players who prefer pristine hulls.

38. **Material sets.** Brass, glass, bone, ceramic and living membrane change appearance
    and sound. Cosmetic variety can be valuable without adding another numerical tier.

39. **A captain's mark.** Put a small player-chosen emblem on the core or hull plating.
    Keep it legible and original, with no dependency on an online account.

40. **A design postcard.** Export a clean image with the ship silhouette, selected loadout
    and its portable design file. Make sharing a creation feel as good as finishing it.

## 41–50: Combat with weight and opportunities

41. **Battery rhythms.** Let selected guns fire together or alternate. A staggered broadside
    can feel different from a simultaneous volley without simply increasing damage.

42. **Meaningful recoil.** A large shot nudges a light ship; a heavy hull absorbs it.
    Movement and fitting choices become visible in the firing behaviour.

43. **Readable disable targets.** Exposed thrusters, sensors and batteries offer choices:
    stop an enemy escaping, silence a dangerous flank or preserve valuable salvage.

44. **Armour facing.** A sturdier side can absorb fire while vulnerable systems are
    repositioned. Telegraph the difference through hull material and impact response.

45. **Use wrecks as cover.** Shots break or push pieces of a wreck. Cover changes during
    a battle, creating routes rather than merely obstructing movement.

46. **A short enemy surrender state.** A crippled ship can power down or flee. The player
    chooses whether to salvage, spare or finish it, with clear consequences.

47. **Enemy roles that cooperate.** A shield carrier protects a gun platform; a repair unit
    tries to retrieve disabled allies. Make the formation readable before it becomes complex.

48. **Large attacks with distinct tells.** Each boss gets a recognisable sound, posture
    and ground pattern for major attacks. Difficulty comes from decisions rather than surprise.

49. **Disabling changes behaviour.** A destroyed flank causes an enemy to turn, retreat
    or improvise. The ship should react to the capability it actually lost.

50. **A clean victory beat.** Use a short flash, a meaningful break-up, scattered salvage
    and musical release. A fixed visual budget keeps large fights from becoming stutters.

## 51–60: Salvage that creates stories

51. **Recognisable objects before labels.** The player can see that a dropped component
    is a gun, reactor or thruster. Preserve the silhouettes learned in the Shipyard.

52. **Strip or destroy.** Careful disabling preserves more usable parts; overwhelming
    force is faster. Both approaches remain valid rather than one being a universal optimum.

53. **A cargo silhouette.** Collected scrap sits visibly in a tow field or cargo cradle.
    Returning home looks and sounds different from leaving with an empty hold.

54. **Interesting damaged parts.** A recovered gun might have a useful quirk and a real
    drawback. Repairs reveal the tradeoff; avoid endless random-stat comparison.

55. **Salvage under a changing hazard.** A reactor is venting, a current is strengthening
    or a patrol is approaching. The player decides which pieces are worth collecting.

56. **A tug and towing tool.** Retrieve awkward objects or reposition a useful wreck.
    Small interactions can support a salvager identity without building a logistics simulator.

57. **Black-box stories.** A recovered recorder adds one short transmission and a bearing.
    The object creates the next expedition rather than a long quest log entry.

58. **Repair a wrecked utility ship.** Bring enough parts to wake a small helper that
    joins for one excursion. It needs a simple role and a visible state, not a management screen.

59. **A satisfying return.** Unloading briefly arranges the recovered pieces on the dock.
    Highlight one interesting find and offer to fit it immediately.

60. **A remembered recovery.** Mark the wreck the player reclaimed and keep their design.
    The game can acknowledge a close escape without imposing punishment timers.

## 61–70: Strange intelligence and environmental storytelling

61. **Signals with habits.** An unknown transmitter repeats with a recognisable cadence.
    Later changes in that cadence can suggest distress, recognition or a warning.

62. **A companion made of recovered machinery.** A small onboard presence comments on
    actual discoveries. Use authored lines and visible triggers instead of an API requirement.

63. **Machines that recognise equipment.** A ruin reacts to a recovered core or a
    particular material family. The player's fitted ship becomes part of exploration.

64. **Non-hostile encounters.** A strange organism observes, imitates or follows the ship.
    Not every unexplained thing needs to become an enemy.

65. **Unreliable transmissions, honest controls.** Lore can be mysterious while targeting,
    damage and interaction prompts remain dependable. Confusion should never masquerade as depth.

66. **Evidence in the arrangement.** A breached shield, inward-facing guns and scattered
    repair parts imply what happened to a site before the player arrived.

67. **A small discovery journal.** Save a picture, one useful fact and the player's note.
    It should be a memory aid, not an encyclopedia required to enjoy the game.

68. **Rare responses to repeated visits.** A persistent signal acknowledges the ship's
    return. Tie the response to an actual saved event rather than inventing familiarity.

69. **Decipher by doing.** Repeated symbols become understandable through interactions.
    Pair an unknown glyph with a physical event before ever explaining it in prose.

70. **An encounter that lets you leave.** A mysterious place can be remembered without
    being completed. Curiosity works better when the game respects the player's exit.

## 71–80: A world that moves without becoming a second job

71. **Seeded local encounters.** Keep the chart stable while encounter formations and
    debris routes vary. Save the seed so a memorable expedition can be shared.

72. **Salvage crews with a purpose.** Neutral workers collect wreckage or repair a beacon.
    A simple visible task makes them feel more alive than aimless background movement.

73. **Patrols that travel.** Enemies move between meaningful locations and leave bearings
    or traces. Give the player a way to avoid or intercept them.

74. **Short distress events.** Rescue a disabled pilot, tow a component or clear a route.
    The event resolves in minutes and does not punish the player for ignoring it.

75. **Local consequences.** Clear an outpost and nearby traffic changes. Let the change
    be visible in the sector rather than only increasing a reputation number.

76. **A temporary travelling merchant.** Meet a trader on an expedition and barter
    immediately. No real-time shop refreshes or daily attendance requirements.

77. **Environmental weather.** A storm alters visibility, drift or sensors for a short
    stretch. Telegraph it from a distance and provide a route around it.

78. **An occasional enormous silhouette.** A distant moving body establishes scale.
    Sometimes it is harmless scenery; sometimes a route lets the player investigate.

79. **A few persistent human-scale relationships.** Recurring dock workers or pilots
    remember specific help. Short lines and changed surroundings carry the relationship.

80. **Instant access to the good part.** Contracts offer clear destinations and immediate
    departure. Keep the sense of a world while preserving arcade immediacy.

## 81–90: Lateral builds and playful experiments

81. **A scavenger fitting.** Trade direct firepower for better towing, collection and
    safe approach tools. It creates a different route through the same encounters.

82. **A broadside fitting.** Side batteries reward positioning and controlled turns.
    The editor makes their arcs visible before launch.

83. **A close-range cutter.** Powerful short-range tools dismantle selected systems but
    demand an approach through hostile arcs. It should remain a risky, readable choice.

84. **A shield-projecting fitting.** Protect a chosen direction, a tow target or an ally.
    Strong defence in one place leaves something else exposed.

85. **A decoy tool.** Distract sensors or lure a patrol away from salvage. Make enemies
    visibly investigate the decoy so the player understands why it worked.

86. **A limited drone bay.** A small number of clear-role helpers support repair or
    retrieval. Hard caps prevent the screen and simulation filling with invisible work.

87. **An unstable core choice.** Add a dramatic benefit with a matching control problem,
    such as recoil, heat or reduced shielding. Describe both sides before installation.

88. **A ram or impact fitting.** Build around committed movement, protection and recovery.
    It needs collision clarity and an escape option, not just a damage multiplier.

89. **Funny functional silhouettes.** Uneven, circular or absurdly long ships can still
    work if their actual parts obey the same rules. Let the player discover the joke.

90. **A quick test excursion.** Launch a new fitting into a short optional scenario and
    return directly to the editor with what happened still visible.

## 91–100: Sharing, comfort and keeping the game small

91. **Portable expedition seeds.** A short code identifies a local scenario and its
    difficulty settings. Let friends compare stories without requiring a live server.

92. **A ship sharing shelf.** Import a design with a preview and explicit confirmation.
    Keep authorship with its creator and preserve the single ship format.

93. **Co-op expeditions later.** Two ships with different roles make towing, shielding and
    salvage naturally social. Prove the single-player loop first, then tackle synchronization.

94. **A simple photo mode.** Hide the HUD, pause and frame the ship. It turns player
    creations and strange places into shareable images without a separate art system.

95. **A short return recap.** Show distance explored, a close escape and one interesting
    find. Avoid grading every quiet trip as an inefficient run.

96. **Remappable inputs.** Make the controls fit the player, including one-handed or
    alternative layouts where practical. Preserve clear defaults.

97. **Readable without colour.** Shapes, motion and sound back up colour-coded threats.
    Let brightness and text size be adjusted independently of gameplay difficulty.

98. **Clear performance settings.** Offer a small quality preset and individual effects
    controls. Keep the important silhouettes, warnings and feedback at every setting.

99. **A genuinely useful pause menu.** Resume, refit, controls, sound, accessibility,
    export and return are easy to find. A browser game should be easy to put down.

100. **A complete small arc.** A first fitting, a short salvage trip, a memorable encounter
     and a meaningful return can form a satisfying game before the universe expands.

## The first atmosphere pass I would actually build

Choose a small connected experience from the menu instead of implementing all 100 ideas:

1. A warm, recognisable home dock with a clear departure.
2. One nearby salvage route with a striking wreck silhouette.
3. A local signal whose sound and light guide the approach.
4. A brief fight where disabling a gun changes the enemy's behaviour.
5. One interesting part worth bringing home and fitting immediately.
6. A different sound bed and silhouette language in the Grave Choir.
7. A quiet return that shows the ship and its recovered cargo.

That creates safety, curiosity, danger and relief with the existing core loop. Test the
whole trip, including ordinary navigation and the return, before adding a new continent
of systems. The player should feel that something happened in five minutes.

## Art to commission first

Prioritise assets that change the experience everywhere they appear:

- A modular home dock kit: entrance, berths, cranes, service lights and repair equipment.
- A small set of large wreck pieces that create traversable silhouettes.
- Distinct material treatments for brass machinery, glass technology and living membrane.
- A few far-background structures per depth, kept separate from interactive objects.
- Clear icons and silhouettes for valuable salvage, danger and navigation.
- Muzzle, impact and engine effects with a shared palette and fixed size budgets.
- A few compact portraits or transmission images for recurring presences.

Choose intended on-screen sizes before producing high-resolution sheets. Preserve clear
mount points and the existing manifest conventions. Cache static scenery; bound particles,
audio voices and active entities. Art should make the game clearer and more evocative at
the same time, without depending on permanent heavy post-processing.
